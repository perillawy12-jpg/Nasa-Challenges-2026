How to read this, and what it leaves out

Plain-English glossary
Height (elevation)
How far above the horizon something is, in degrees. 0° is the horizon, 90° is straight up. Here the Sun never gets above about 1.5°.
Direction (azimuth)
The compass direction: north is 0°, east is 90°, south is 180°, west is 270°.
Power
How much sunlight reaches the solar panels. 100% means the whole Sun disc is above the horizon all the time.
Dark spell
A stretch of time with no Sun, so no solar power. Batteries have to last through it.
No contact
A stretch of time with Earth below the horizon, so no direct radio link.
Good start
A day to land and start the mission so that the Sun stays up for the whole mission and Earth is in view as much as you asked for.
Why is the Sun so low?
The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5°, so near its poles the Sun just circles along the horizon once every 29.5 days.
Reading the charts

Each line shows how far the center of the Sun or Earth sits above the local horizon. Above zero it is in view. The bars underneath show what share of each pixel's time span the body is up, so brighter means more. Near the pole the Sun circles the horizon once a lunar day (about 29.5 Earth days) and never climbs more than about 1.5° above it, which is why small terrain or mast changes matter so much. Earth's disc in the sky view shows its real phase, which is the opposite of the Moon's phase seen from Earth.

Why sites look alike on a smooth Moon

Within about a degree of the pole, the Sun and Earth angles on a perfectly smooth sphere are almost identical for every site. Real differences come from ridges and rims. Use the terrain slider, a mast height, or a measured horizon profile to see them.

Check against NASA JPL Horizons

Reference values: NASA/JPL Horizons (ephemeris DE441), target Moon (301), geocentric observer, 25 dates from 1 Jan to 27 Dec 2026 at 00:00 UT, quantities ObsSub and SunSub in the Moon's mean-Earth frame. Our values come from the same engine that drives the page. The Earth latitude shows a small steady offset of about 0.02°; we have not tracked down its cause.

Method and accuracy
  • Moon position from the Meeus lunar series, Sun from the low-precision solar formula, orientation from Meeus chapter 53 optical and physical libration, with times converted from UTC to terrestrial time.
  • Checked against NASA/JPL Horizons on 25 dates in 2026: the sub-Earth and sub-solar points agree within about 0.03° (see the check above). The Moon position reproduces the published Meeus worked example to about 0.001°. Libration agrees with that example to a few hundredths of a degree. At the pole the Sun stays within about ±1.6° and Earth within about ±7° of the horizon, as expected.
  • The Moon is a 1737.4 km sphere. Earth's position includes parallax from the lander. The Sun counts as up when its center clears the effective horizon. The power index gives partial credit while the disc is rising or setting.
  • NASA's animation prints no dates. The dates used here (1 Jan to 30 Apr 2026, one hour of Moon time per video frame) were worked out by matching Earth's phase, left-right drift and up-down bob against the ephemeris above. The match is strong (correlation about 0.99 on both drifts) and the pixel scales agree with Earth's size, but treat the dates as inferred, good to about half a day, not official. Outside those dates the panel shows the closest look-alike moment: the Sun's direction is usually within about 1°, Earth's position within about 3°, and rarely off by up to 8°.
  • Keyboard: left and right arrows move the time marker one step (Shift for ten), Space plays or pauses, Home and End jump to the ends of the window.
  • Not modeled: real terrain, Earth ground stations (a clear Earth in the lunar sky is necessary, not sufficient), spacecraft pointing, eclipses of the Sun by Earth, and dust or thermal limits.
